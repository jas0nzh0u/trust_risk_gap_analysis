ANALYSER.IPYNB - DETAILED CELL-BY-CELL EXPLANATION
===================================================

This notebook analyses survey data about trust and risk perceptions across
Snapchat, Facebook and WhatsApp. It loads raw survey responses, cleans them,
builds composite scores, and produces six charts. Below is a plain-English
explanation of every cell.

Note on question numbering: Question 9 is not present in the survey export.
The numbering jumps from Q8 to Q10. This is intentional and reflected
throughout the code.

What it does:

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL 1 - Dummy-encode multi-select questions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    def dummy_encode_multiselect(df, columns):
        for col in columns:
            dummies = df[col].astype(str).str.get_dummies(sep=";")
            dummies.columns = [f"{col}_{opt.strip()}" for opt in dummies.columns]
            df = pd.concat([df, dummies], axis=1).drop(columns=[col])
        return df

    df.columns = df.columns.str.strip()
    multi_select_cols = [col for col in df.columns
                         if df[col].astype(str).str.contains(";").any()]
    df = dummy_encode_multiselect(df, multi_select_cols)
    print(f"After dummy encoding: {len(df.columns)} columns")

What it does:
    Some survey questions let respondents pick more than one answer (e.g.
    "Select all that apply"). In the CSV, these answers are stored in a
    single cell separated by semicolons, like "Facebook;WhatsApp".

    This cell splits those combined answers into separate yes/no columns.
    For example, a column containing "Facebook;WhatsApp" becomes two new
    columns:
        - "..._Facebook" = 1
        - "..._WhatsApp" = 1
        - "..._Snapchat" = 0  (because the respondent did not pick it)

    Step by step:
    1. df.columns.str.strip() removes any accidental spaces around column
       names.
    2. It finds every column where at least one cell contains a semicolon -
       these are the multi-select questions.
    3. The dummy_encode_multiselect function loops through each of those
       columns, splits the semicolon-separated values into individual 0/1
       columns, attaches them to the dataframe, and removes the original
       combined column.

    This is necessary because you cannot do maths on text like
    "Facebook;WhatsApp", but you can work with 0s and 1s.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL 3 - Convert Likert text responses to numbers
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    likert_map = {
        "Never": 1, "Rarely": 2, "Sometimes": 3, "Often": 4, "Always": 5,
        "Not confident at all": 1, "Not very confident": 2, "Neutral": 3,
        "Confident": 4, "Very confident": 5,
        "Not safe at all": 1, "Not very safe": 2, "Safe": 4, "Very safe": 5,
        "Every day": 5, "Few times a week": 4, "Once a week or less": 3,
        "Yes, I was aware of this": 1, "No, I was not aware of this": 0,
    }

    for col in likert_cols:
        df[col] = df[col].map(likert_map)

What it does:
    Many survey questions use a "Likert scale" - a range of text options from
    low to high (e.g. "Never" to "Always", or "Not safe at all" to
    "Very safe"). To do any maths or comparisons, these text answers need to
    become numbers.

    The likert_map dictionary defines the conversion:
        "Never"             -> 1  (lowest)
        "Rarely"            -> 2
        "Sometimes"         -> 3
        "Often"             -> 4
        "Always"            -> 5  (highest)

    Similar scales exist for confidence (1-5), safety (1-5), and frequency
    of platform use (1-5). Location awareness is binary: aware = 1, not
    aware = 0.

    The code loops through every Likert column (Q4, Q5, Q7, Q11, Q12, Q13)
    and replaces the text with the corresponding number using .map().

    After this cell, every value in these columns is a number, which means
    we can calculate averages, differences, and correlations.

CELL 4 - Compute composite scores
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    df["platform_awareness_score"] = ( ... ) / 4
    df["privacy_behaviour_score"] = ( ... ) / 6
    df["vulnerability_exposure_score"] = ( ... ) / 6
    df["trust_risk_gap_snapchat"] = df["Q12_snapchat"] - df["Q13_snapchat"]
    ...
    df["trust_risk_gap_mean"] = ( ... ) / 3
    df["platform_comparison_score"] = ( ... ) / 8

What it does:
    Individual question scores are useful, but to see bigger patterns we
    combine related questions into "composite scores". Each composite score
    is the average of several related questions, giving one number that
    summarises a broader concept.

    The five composite scores are:

    1. Platform Awareness Score (average of Q5 + Q7)
       How aware the user is of platform risks. Combines:
       - Q5: whether they knew platforms share location data (0 or 1)
       - Q7: confidence in recognising grooming on each platform (1-5)
       A higher score means more awareness.

    2. Privacy Behaviour Score (average of Q11 + Q13)
       How actively the user manages their privacy. Combines:
       - Q11: how often they review privacy settings (1-5 per platform)
       - Q13: how often they accept stranger requests (1-5 per platform)
       A higher score means more active privacy management (but note that
       a high Q13 means they accept strangers MORE often, which is risky).

    3. Vulnerability Exposure Score (average of Q10 + Q13)
       How exposed the user is to risk. Combines:
       - Q10: whether they have received uncomfortable messages (0 or 1
         per platform)
       - Q13: how often they accept stranger requests (1-5 per platform)
       A higher score means greater exposure to risk.

    4. Trust-Risk Gap Score (Q12 minus Q13, per platform and overall mean)
       The core metric of the project. For each platform, this subtracts
       the stranger contact frequency (Q13, a risk behaviour) from the
       perceived safety score (Q12, a trust feeling).

       Example: if someone feels "Safe" on Snapchat (Q12 = 4) but "Often"
       accepts strangers (Q13 = 4), the gap is 0 - they behave as
       cautiously as they feel safe.

       But if they feel "Safe" (4) yet "Rarely" accept strangers (2), the
       gap is +2 - they are more trusting than their behaviour suggests.

       A large positive gap means the user trusts the platform much more
       than their actual behaviour would warrant, which is the "trust-risk
       gap" the project investigates.

       trust_risk_gap_mean is the average of the three per-platform gaps.

    5. Platform Comparison Score (average of Q6 + Q8 + Q14)
       How respondents compare platforms against each other. Combines:
       - Q6: which platform is hardest to report abuse on (0 or 1)
       - Q8: which platform is most likely for grooming (0 or 1)
       - Q14: which platform offers best protection (0 or 1)
       A higher score means more respondents flagged that platform.

    .describe() at the end prints summary statistics (count, mean, std,
    min, max, etc.) for all five composite scores.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL 7 - Chart 1: Mean trust-risk gap by platform
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

What it does:
    Creates a bar chart with three bars - one for Snapchat, Facebook, and
    WhatsApp - showing the average trust-risk gap score for each platform.

    Key details:
    - Each bar's height is the mean (average) of that platform's trust-risk
      gap across all respondents.
    - Error bars show the standard error of the mean (SEM), which indicates
      how confident we can be in the average. Shorter error bars mean more
      confidence. SEM is calculated as standard deviation divided by the
      square root of the sample size.
    - Bars are coloured with each platform's brand colour.
    - A dashed grey line at y=0 shows the baseline (no gap).
    - The chart is saved as "chart1_trust_risk_gap_by_platform.png" at
      150 DPI (high quality for reports).

    What to look for:
    - Which platform has the largest gap? That is where users' trust most
      exceeds their caution.
    - Do error bars overlap between platforms? If they do, the difference
      may not be statistically meaningful with this sample size.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL 8 - Chart 2: Correlation heatmap
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

What it does:
    Creates a triangular heatmap showing how strongly each pair of composite
    scores is related (correlated).

    Key details:
    - A correlation ranges from -1 to +1:
        +1 = perfect positive relationship (when one goes up, the other
             always goes up)
         0 = no relationship
        -1 = perfect negative relationship (when one goes up, the other
             always goes down)
    - The heatmap uses the "coolwarm" colour scheme: red/warm = positive
      correlation, blue/cool = negative correlation, white = near zero.
    - Only the lower triangle is shown (the upper triangle is a mirror
      image, so hiding it removes redundancy).
    - Each cell displays the correlation value to two decimal places.
    - df[available].corr() computes the Pearson correlation coefficient
      between every pair of composite scores.

    What to look for:
    - Strong positive correlations (close to +1) between scores suggest
      those concepts move together in your respondents.
    - Negative correlations would mean as one increases the other decreases.
    - Very low correlations (near 0) suggest the scores measure independent
      things, which is good for having distinct composite scores.
